## Editor's note

What follows is a newspaper editorial that ran on the leader page of *The Sun* of New York on Tuesday, 21 September 1897. It was unsigned, as editorials were. It is about five hundred words long. It is the most reprinted editorial ever to appear in an English-language newspaper,[^reprint] and it has never gone out of print in a hundred and twenty-nine years.

It is reproduced here in full and unaltered, spelling and small capitals as printed. The commentary is ours and sits after it, so that anyone who wants only the thing itself can read it and leave.

One detail before you start, because it changes how the piece reads. It is not a Christmas editorial. It ran in September.

---

## The text

*The Sun*, New York, 21 September 1897.

> **IS THERE A SANTA CLAUS?**
>
> We take pleasure in answering at once and thus prominently the communication below, expressing at the same time our great gratification that its faithful author is numbered among the friends of THE SUN:

> *"Dear Editor: I am 8 years old.*
> *"Some of my little friends say there is no Santa Claus.*
> *"Papa says 'If you see it in THE SUN it's so.'*
> *"Please tell me the truth: is there a Santa Claus?*
>
> *"Virginia O'Hanlon.*
> *"115 West Ninety-fifth street."*

VIRGINIA, your little friends are wrong. They have been affected by the skepticism of a skeptical age. They do not believe except they see. They think that nothing can be which is not comprehensible by their little minds. All minds, VIRGINIA, whether they be men's or children's, are little. In this great universe of ours man is a mere insect, an ant, in his intellect, as compared with the boundless world about him, as measured by the intelligence capable of grasping the whole of truth and knowledge.

Yes, VIRGINIA, there is a Santa Claus. He exists as certainly as love and generosity and devotion exist, and you know that they abound and give to your life its highest beauty and joy. Alas! how dreary would be the world if there were no Santa Claus. It would be as dreary as if there were no VIRGINIAS. There would be no childlike faith then, no poetry, no romance to make tolerable this existence. We should have no enjoyment, except in sense and sight. The eternal light with which childhood fills the world would be extinguished.

Not believe in Santa Claus! You might as well not believe in fairies! You might get your papa to hire men to watch in all the chimneys on Christmas Eve to catch Santa Claus, but even if they did not see Santa Claus coming down, what would that prove? Nobody sees Santa Claus, but that is no sign that there is no Santa Claus. The most real things in the world are those that neither children nor men can see. Did you ever see fairies dancing on the lawn? Of course not, but that's no proof that they are not there. Nobody can conceive or imagine all the wonders there are unseen and unseeable in the world.

You may tear apart the baby's rattle and see what makes the noise inside, but there is a veil covering the unseen world which not the strongest man, nor even the united strength of all the strongest men that ever lived, could tear apart. Only faith, fancy, poetry, love, romance, can push aside that curtain and view and picture the supernal beauty and glory beyond. Is it all real? Ah, VIRGINIA, in all this world there is nothing else real and abiding.

No Santa Claus! Thank GOD! he lives, and he lives forever. A thousand years from now, VIRGINIA, nay, ten times ten thousand years from now, he will continue to make glad the heart of childhood.

---

## Notes on the text

### The girl

Laura Virginia O'Hanlon was born in New York on 20 July 1889 and was eight when she wrote.[^vo] Her father, Dr Philip O'Hanlon, was an assistant to the coroner on the Upper West Side. When she asked him whether Santa Claus was real, he did what a certain kind of nineteenth-century parent did with a hard question: he referred it upward. *If you see it in The Sun, it's so.*

She did not stay eight. She took a bachelor's degree from Hunter College in 1910, a master's from Columbia in 1912, and a doctorate from Fordham. She entered the New York City schools as a teacher the year she finished her master's and became a junior principal in 1935. She taught for forty-seven years and died on 13 May 1971, having spent a good part of her adult life answering letters about a letter she wrote when she was eight.

### The writer

The editorial was written by Francis Pharcellus Church, born in Rochester on 22 February 1839. He had covered the Civil War as a correspondent before joining *The Sun*, where he wrote leaders anonymously for two decades. He was, by the accounts that survive, not a sentimental man and not obviously the person you would hand a letter from a child.

#### The twenty anonymous years

Nobody knew he had written it. Editorials in that period carried no byline — the paper spoke, not the person — and Church went on writing them for another nine years without claiming the most famous piece of prose he would ever produce. His authorship was disclosed only after he died, on 11 April 1906.

There is something in that worth sitting with. The most reprinted editorial in the language was written by a man who never once, in public, said he wrote it.

### The paper

*The Sun* was founded on 3 September 1833 by Benjamin Day, the first successful penny daily in America, and within a year it had the largest circulation in the country. Charles A. Dana bought it in 1868 and edited it for twenty-nine years; 1897 was the last of them.

It outlived everyone in this story except Virginia. On 5 January 1950 it merged into the *New York World-Telegram* and the name came off the masthead after a hundred and sixteen years.

## What it does that opinion writing mostly does not

Read the piece again with an editor's eye and it is doing three things very deliberately, none of which are common now.

### It concedes the objection first, and completely

The second sentence gives the sceptics their strongest ground: *they do not believe except they see*. It does not caricature them. It does not call them cynics. It states their position in a form they would recognise and accept.

Then it turns, and the turn works precisely because the concession was honest. Modern opinion writing almost always states the opposing view in the weakest form it can get away with, which feels like winning and reads like fear.

### It never once says "I"

Not a single first-person singular in five hundred words. The paper is speaking — *we take pleasure in answering* — and the argument is carried entirely by the thing being argued rather than by the standing of the person arguing it.

This is a lost register. An enormous amount of what is published as opinion today is really testimony: here is my experience, here is my credential, therefore believe me. Church had no credential to offer an eight-year-old and did not look for one.

### It ends on a cadence rather than a conclusion

Look at the last sentence. It does not summarise, it does not call anyone to action, it does not tell you what to think. It lengthens:

> A thousand years from now, VIRGINIA, nay, ten times ten thousand years from now, he will continue to make glad the heart of childhood.

The rhythm does the closing. *A thousand years* is a long span; *ten times ten thousand years* is an absurd one, and the absurdity is the point — the sentence physically opens out as you read it. Say it aloud and you can hear where the writer put the breath.

Three sentences earlier he had written the shortest line in the piece: *Is it all real?* Four words after a sixty-word sentence. That is not an accident either.

## The afterlife of five hundred words

| Year | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 1897 | Runs unsigned in *The Sun*, 21 September, on the leader page |
| 1906 | Church dies; his authorship is disclosed |
| 1950 | *The Sun* merges into the *New York World-Telegram*, 5 January |
| 1971 | Virginia O'Hanlon Douglas dies, aged 81 |
| Since | Translated into some twenty languages, set to music, staged, filmed, and reprinted every December somewhere in the world |

It has now been in print for longer than the newspaper that printed it existed.

## Why reprint it here

Partly because it is beautiful and out of copyright and nobody needs a reason to reprint a beautiful thing.

Mostly because it is a useful yardstick. Five hundred words, no byline, no data, no call to action, written to an audience of one and read by everybody, and it has outlasted the paper, the editor, the writer, the girl, and the century. If a piece of writing can do that with a letter from an eight-year-old and no expertise at all, the bar was never sources or credentials or length.

It was rhythm, and honesty about the other side, and knowing exactly where to stop.

[^reprint]: The claim is standard in accounts of the editorial and repeated by the Library of Congress in its guide to the piece in *Chronicling America*. It is not a claim anyone has audited, and it would be an interesting thing to try to audit.

[^vo]: She published later in life under her married name, Virginia O'Hanlon Douglas.
